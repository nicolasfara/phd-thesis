\chapter{Deployment and Reconfiguration Strategies on the Edge-Cloud Continuum}
\label{chap:deployment-and-reconfiguration}

The deployment of a macro-program is deliberately underdetermined:
\cref{thm:deployment-independence} guarantees that every correct deployment of a well-formed macro-program computes the same thing,
so the assignment of components to hosts is free to be chosen -- and later revised -- on non-functional grounds alone, without touching the application logic (\cref{sec:pulverization-discussion}).
%
On the language side, the gap is already closed:
aggregate computing, choreographic programming, and multitier programming can all be made to produce the deployable components the model expects (\cref{chap:language-and-coordination}).
%
Neither says who exercises that freedom, or how the resulting deployment is kept good as the infrastructure moves under it.

That question is an explicit runtime control problem.
%
Two decisions have to be made whichever way the question is answered.
%
The first is what a deployment is for:
which non-functional quantity -- energy drawn, battery remaining, carbon emitted -- a candidate deployment should improve, against what it is allowed to cost in latency or communication overhead.
%
The second is how often, and by what mechanism, a deployment already running is revisited:
continuously, in reaction to a local signal, or periodically, by recomputing the assignment from scratch against the infrastructure's current state.

Both questions are answered three times, at three points of the same design space.
%
The first states the decision as a single rule, triggered locally:
a device offloads a specific, resource-heavy component to a nearby host the moment its own battery drops below a threshold, and moves it again if that host becomes unreachable,
with no search involved and nothing stated beyond the threshold itself (\cref{sec:battery-triggered-offloading}).
%
The second states the decision as a self-organising rule too, but a harder one:
infrastructural devices compete for the components application devices would rather not run locally by resizing, through a field built collectively, the region each one leads,
so a device ends up offloaded to whichever infrastructural device currently combines proximity with spare capacity (\cref{sec:global-self-organising-reconfiguration}).
%
The third states the same decision declaratively instead:
an explicit objective over energy and carbon is given once, together with the hardware and latency requirements of every component,
and a planner searches the space of admissible deployments for one that meets it, repeating the search as the network drifts (\cref{sec:declarative-green-planning}).
%
All three policies are evaluated against a comparable currency and set against each other (\cref{sec:deployment-reconfiguration-discussion}):
how much of a targeted non-functional gain -- energy, battery life, carbon, or the share of devices able to offload at all -- they buy, and how much of it costs again in extra hops, extra latency, or a slower transient before the gain materialises.

\section{A Self-organising Offloading Rule Triggered by Battery Level}
\label{sec:battery-triggered-offloading}

The rescue scenario of \cref{fig:rescue-case-study} supplies the evaluation's macro-program,
narrowed to the two components whose interaction the offloading decision actually affects:
Emergency Service and Steering Service, both collective~\cite{DBLP:conf/acsos/FarabegoliVC24}.
%
Biometrics Acquisition, Navigation System, and Data Analysis are left out,
since none of them is a candidate for offloading in what follows.
%
Emergency Service is the expensive one:
locating an emergency from neighbouring devices' readings costs far more than Steering Service's job of guiding a rescuer once a location is known, whose computational cost is negligible.
%
The rule this section evaluates accordingly only ever moves Emergency Service; Steering Service always stays at its owner.

In the monolithic deployment -- the same baseline \cref{subsec:pulverization-validation} used to check deployment independence -- both components run at their owner.
%
The deployment evaluated here instead offloads Emergency Service opportunistically.
%
Each device starts a run with a battery level drawn at random,
and the moment that level falls below 30\%,
the device forwards its Emergency Service instance to the nearest edge server acting as its surrogate (\cref{subsec:forwarding-chains}),
switching to a different surrogate if the connection to the current one is lost.
%
No other condition drives the decision:
not load, not the state of the network elsewhere, only the device's own battery.
%
This is what makes the rule self-organising in the narrowest sense:
each device decides for itself, from one locally observed number, with no negotiation and no view of what any other device is doing.

Interpreting the result needs a power model for the two device classes involved.
%
The reference values come from a smartphone and an edge server:
a Snapdragon 888, with a thermal design power of 5 W and an energy cost of 0.55 J per instruction,
and a Xeon Platinum P-8124, with a thermal design power of 220 W and an energy cost of 10 J per instruction,
drawn from Shao and Brooks's published per-instruction energy measurements for both device classes~\cite{DBLP:conf/islped/ShaoB13}.
%
A 4500 mAh battery at 3.7 V then fixes the energy budget each device starts a run with.

The same three network sizes \cref{subsec:pulverization-validation} used to validate deployment independence -- 50, 100, and 200 mobile devices -- are reused here,
simulated again in Alchemist~\cite{DBLP:journals/jos/PianiniMV13} with eight edge servers and one cloud instance available as surrogates,
under the mobility pattern already described for the functional validation.
%
\Cref{fig:power-consumption} compares the monolithic deployment against the one that offloads Emergency Service under the battery rule.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{power-consumption.pdf}
    \caption[Battery level and message overhead under a monolithic and an offloaded deployment]{Average battery level (left) and cumulative messages exchanged (right) by application devices, under a monolithic and an offloaded deployment. Data from~\cite{DBLP:conf/acsos/FarabegoliVC24}.}
    \label{fig:power-consumption}
\end{figure}

The offloaded deployment leaves devices with more remaining battery over the run.
%
It does so at the cost of a modest increase in the number of messages exchanged,
visible in the right-hand chart of \cref{fig:power-consumption}:
reaching Emergency Service at its surrogate and returning its result takes hops that a local execution would not need.
%
The two charts together say where that cost lands.
%
It is not paid in energy -- the offloaded devices are the ones with more battery left, not less -- but in bandwidth,
in the extra messages the forwarding chain to the surrogate requires.

The rule buys this result with the cheapest possible statement:
one threshold, one component, and no coordination between devices.
%
What it cannot do is generalise beyond the one signal and the one component it was written for.
%
A second bottleneck, or a second component worth offloading under different conditions, would need a second threshold chosen by hand.

\section{A Field-based Reconfiguration Policy over Self-organising Coordination Regions}
\label{sec:global-self-organising-reconfiguration}

The policy evaluated in this section answers the same offloading question as \cref{sec:battery-triggered-offloading} --
which infrastructural device should run the component an application device would rather not run locally --
but the decision is no longer read off a single, locally observed number.
%
Every infrastructural device seeds a potential field weighted by its own current load,
so a lightly loaded device's field reaches further into the network than a heavily loaded one's,
and each application device is claimed by whichever field reaches it with the lowest potential~\cite{DBLP:journals/iot/FarabegoliPCV24}.
%
The construction is the self-organising coordination regions pattern already met in \cref{subsec:pulverization-validation}~\cite{DBLP:journals/fgcs/PianiniCVN21},
with one difference:
instead of electing leaders at a fixed grain, every infrastructural device already qualifies as one,
and what its region-forming field encodes is not distance alone but the leader's own current load,
so a leader carrying more work commands a smaller region.
%
A converge-cast brings each region's candidate components to its leader;
the leader accepts as many as its own capacity still allows and casts the decision back over the same region,
so every application device ends up either keeping its component locally or running it on the infrastructural device that claimed it.
%
Because the regions resize themselves as load shifts, the policy reasons from a global stance over the whole network's own topology,
rather than from the single local signal \cref{sec:battery-triggered-offloading} relies on,
while still running over the same infrastructure/application-device split \cref{chap:pulverization}'s middleware maintains.

The policy is evaluated against a local baseline that pins every application device to its nearest infrastructural device by hop count, irrespective of that device's load,
over networks of a thousand hosts arranged as a scale-free and as a lobster, tree-like topology,
under two scenarios: a variable load imposed externally on infrastructural devices, and a graceful degradation and recovery in which batches of infrastructural devices are switched off and then progressively reactivated.
%
Quality of service is measured as the share of application devices that manage to offload their component at all.
%
Across both scenarios and both topologies, the field-based policy reaches a higher quality of service than the local baseline once its regions have stabilised,
but stabilisation itself takes time, and quality of service can briefly dip below the baseline's during that transient before recovering past it.
%
The policy also degrades and recovers more gracefully than the baseline when infrastructural devices fail.
%
Both advantages are more pronounced in the scale-free topology than in the lobster one:
removing a node from a lobster network is far more likely to segment it,
cutting off exactly the alternative routes the field-based policy relies on to redirect load elsewhere~\cite{DBLP:journals/iot/FarabegoliPCV24}.

\section{A Declarative Planner for Green Deployments}
\label{sec:declarative-green-planning}

\Cref{subsec:declarative-deployment} left declarative continuous reasoning at the level of Forti and Brogi's incremental repair of a stated deployment goal~\cite{DBLP:journals/logcom/FortiBB22}.
%
The planner presented here descends from the same idea, worked out over the deployment units \cref{chap:pulverization} defines~\cite{DBLP:conf/coordination/BrogiCFFV25}.
%
Every deployment unit declares its hardware requirements and a maximum latency it tolerates towards whichever other unit of the same macro-program it depends on most closely;
every candidate host declares its available and total capacity, the power-usage effectiveness of running work on it, and the mix of energy sources -- and the associated carbon intensity -- that supplies it.
%
A Prolog-based generator searches this space for an assignment of every deployment unit to a host that meets both kinds of requirement, stated as facts rather than computed by a bespoke solver, and resolved by ordinary logic-program resolution.
%
Because the assignment problem is the same combinatorial one \cref{subsec:constraint-based-placement} already found too costly to solve exactly at scale,
the generator orders candidate hosts from the least to the most carbon-intensive first,
so the search reaches a low-carbon assignment quickly instead of exploring the space exhaustively.
%
For every candidate it considers, the same declarative program estimates the resulting energy draw and carbon emissions, from each host's load before and after the candidate placement, its power-usage effectiveness, and its energy mix's carbon intensity.

The generator is invoked from inside Alchemist~\cite{DBLP:journals/jos/PianiniMV13}, the same simulator \cref{subsec:pulverization-validation} used to validate the model,
at a fixed interval.
%
Each time it runs, it is handed the infrastructure's current state and returns a new assignment,
so a deployment already running is periodically replaced by whichever one the search currently favours --
the same kind of repeated, declarative repair \cref{subsec:declarative-deployment} introduced, here driven by an explicit energy and carbon objective rather than by an incremental reasoner reacting to a stated goal.

One instantiation detail is worth stating plainly, once.
%
The published planner states its requirements over five deployment units per device -- sensing, actuation, behaviour, state, and communication -- the fixed partitioning of a logical device \cref{sec:pulverization-motivation} recalls as this model's predecessor,
with the latency bound stated towards the state unit specifically, since the other four of the five were designed to depend on it.
%
Nothing about the search itself is tied to that count or to a single hub:
a hardware requirement and a latency bound towards whatever a unit actually depends on are exactly the kind of per-component declaration the bindings of \cref{subsec:macroprogram-dag} already make explicit for a macro-program of any size,
and the same Prolog predicates would run over a \ac{DAG} of arbitrarily many components without change.
%
What is evaluated below, however, is the five-role instantiation, not a macro-program pulverised into a \ac{DAG} of arbitrarily many components.
%
The mechanism generalises; the reported numbers do not, on their own, speak for deployments finer than five units per device.

The evaluation is synthetic.
%
A variable number of physical devices, each capable of running every component locally, are placed uniformly at random in a two-dimensional area whose size scales with device count,
connected to one another within a range that also grows with device count -- a stand-in for a short-range radio -- and all connected to a single cloud instance.
%
Each device's mix of energy sources follows a jittered sine wave, capped at 90\% green energy for devices and 50\% for the cloud,
standing in for the day-night cycle in a grid's own carbon intensity, the same time-varying supply \cref{subsec:energy-aware-orchestration} raised for Wiesner et al.'s carbon-aware scheduling~\cite{DBLP:conf/middleware/WiesnerBSGT21}.
%
Each run simulates 720 simulated minutes of device movement, repeated ten times per configuration to average out random placement and mobility.
%
Two deployments are compared over the same runs:
a peer-to-peer baseline that keeps every unit at its owner for the whole run -- the monolithic deployment in this thesis's terms --
against a deployment the generator recomputes every 30 simulated minutes.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{green-planner-carbon-energy.pdf}
    \caption[Energy consumption and carbon footprint under a peer-to-peer baseline and the declarative planner]{Overall energy consumption (orange) and carbon footprint (blue) across network sizes, comparing the peer-to-peer baseline (top row) with the deployment recomputed every 30 simulated minutes by the declarative planner (bottom row). Shaded bands show one standard deviation across ten repetitions. Data from~\cite{DBLP:conf/coordination/BrogiCFFV25}.}
    \label{fig:green-planner-carbon-energy}
\end{figure}

At every network size tested, the deployment the planner produces draws roughly a third of the energy the peer-to-peer baseline does for the same network --
under 2 kWh for 50 devices, rising to 3.5 kWh for 100 -- and its carbon footprint follows the same pattern (\cref{fig:green-planner-carbon-energy}).
%
Because each device's energy mix drifts along a sinusoid, the peer-to-peer baseline's carbon footprint tracks that sinusoid exactly:
its deployment never changes, so it cannot exploit a momentarily greener mix elsewhere in the network.
%
The planner's footprint departs from that curve.
%
Recomputing the assignment every 30 minutes lets it keep steering deployment units towards whichever host's energy mix is least carbon-intensive at the time,
realising for a pulverised deployment the same time-varying objective \cref{subsec:energy-aware-orchestration} described for a single scheduled job.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{green-planner-latencies.pdf}
    \caption[Intra-component and inter-device latency under a peer-to-peer baseline and the declarative planner]{Intra-component latency (blue), the average delay a device experiences reaching its own deployment units wherever they have been placed, and inter-device latency (orange), the delay between the communication units of neighbouring devices, across network sizes, for the peer-to-peer baseline and the declarative planner. Shaded bands show one standard deviation across ten repetitions. Data from~\cite{DBLP:conf/coordination/BrogiCFFV25}.}
    \label{fig:green-planner-latencies}
\end{figure}

The energy reduction of \cref{fig:green-planner-carbon-energy} is not free, and \cref{fig:green-planner-latencies} reports where it is spent.
%
Intra-component latency -- the delay a device experiences reaching its own deployment units, now spread across more than one host -- rises well above the near-zero, in-memory cost of the peer-to-peer baseline,
which is the expected price of leaving units off the device that owns them.
%
That increase, though, stays within the same order of magnitude as the network grows,
evidence that the search keeps a device's units close together rather than scattering them further as the problem gets larger.
%
Inter-device latency -- the delay between the communication units of neighbouring devices, the one that matters directly for the collective computation itself -- rises only slightly,
even though nothing in the objective explicitly rewards keeping communicating units nearby;
the same tendency to concentrate a device's units on a small number of nearby hosts, rather than to solve for communication latency directly, is what keeps neighbouring devices' communication units close to one another too.
%
The search itself scales well:
computing a new assignment takes on the order of 100 ms regardless of network size, well inside the 30-minute interval between successive replanning rounds~\cite{DBLP:conf/coordination/BrogiCFFV25}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:deployment-reconfiguration-discussion}

The three evaluations agree on more than they disagree on.
%
All three show that revising who runs a component at runtime buys a real reduction in some targeted quantity at a bounded, secondary cost --
more messages for the offloading rule, more latency for the planner, a stabilisation transient for the field-based policy -- rather than trading one non-functional gain for another outright.
%
None of the three results, however, removes the work of deciding what counts as a good deployment in the first place.
%
The offloading rule needs a person to fix a battery threshold and to decide, in advance, which one component is worth offloading.
%
The field-based policy needs a person to decide that load, specifically, is the quantity its regions should compete over.
%
The planner needs a person to state the energy and carbon objective and the constraints the search should respect, and to decide how often the search should be repeated.
%
All three policies are, in this sense, written by hand:
one as a single number, tripped locally and without coordination;
one as a field, resized competitively by the infrastructural devices themselves;
the third as a declarative statement, solved centrally and repeated on a fixed schedule.

What differs between them is where that judgement is exercised, and how far it reaches.
%
A single threshold says nothing about a second bottleneck, a second component, or a trade-off between two competing objectives;
extending the rule means writing a new rule.
%
The field-based policy already reasons over the whole network's topology, and recovers from a failed host better than either alternative does,
but that recovery is itself at the mercy of the topology it runs on --
a benefit the threshold rule and the planner, tested over a single topology each, do not have to earn.
%
The planner's objective already accommodates several concerns at once -- energy, carbon, a latency bound enforced as a hard constraint --
but only because someone wrote out that objective and those constraints beforehand,
and adding a further concern is still a matter of stating it by hand.

\Cref{chap:heterogeneous-gnn} takes up what happens once even that requirement is dropped:
instead of a rule or an objective fixed in advance,
a policy is trained to weigh the same non-functional concerns from experience,
over a condition -- network congestion -- that none of the three policies of this chapter was built to see.
