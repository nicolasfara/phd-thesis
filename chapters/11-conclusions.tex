\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{chap:conclusions}

Deploying a collective computation means assigning its components to execution hosts.
A deployment descriptor commonly fixes this assignment before execution,
using the infrastructure available at the time.
On the edge-cloud continuum, however, host capabilities span orders of magnitude,
and mobility, battery depletion, and connectivity loss change which hosts remain available.
An assignment that initially meets the application's requirements may later become inefficient or infeasible.

Under the two conditions in \cref{thm:deployment-independence},
a macro-program partitioned along its data flow produces the same stabilised output under every correct deployment.
%
This gives a policy room to choose and revise component placement according to non-functional criteria.
The thesis develops language support for expressing these components across coordination paradigms
and runtime policies for choosing among admissible deployments.
It also investigates how aggregate programs can be generated from natural-language specifications.

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{sec:rq-answers}

\subsection{Deployment Model}
\label{subsec:answer-model}

Deployment independence answers \cref{rq:model} under two conditions:
every component implements a self-stabilising computation,
and sensor state is uniform along every forwarding chain.
The partition assigns one deployable component to each node of the macro-program's data-flow \ac{DAG} (\cref{subsec:macroprogram-dag}).
When both conditions hold, the values at the global output ports stabilise to the same result
regardless of how a correct deployment maps the components onto physical devices.

The proof of \cref{thm:deployment-independence} treats the extra hops in a forwarding chain as a transient perturbation
that the components' self-stabilisation absorbs.
%
The theorem concerns eventual behaviour; it does not bound the difference between deployment outputs before convergence.
Simulations examined this transient for a gradient and a region-partitioning service
on networks of 50, 100, and 200 mobile nodes,
comparing a monolithic deployment with one that offloads components to infrastructural devices.
The outputs remain close while the nodes move.
Once movement stops, their difference falls to a small residual, consistent with self-stabilisation (\cref{subsec:pulverization-validation}).

The hardware demonstrator implements one admissible deployment:
a server evaluates an aggregate program for nine robots,
each carrying an ESP32 microcontroller without an aggregate runtime.
A camera above the arena supplies the positions the robots cannot sense themselves (\cref{sec:emerge-as-pulverisation}).
This ceiling-based sensing satisfies condition \emph{(ii)} of the theorem.
The deployment was fixed by hand before execution;
no policy revised it, and the study reported no measurements (\cref{sec:emerge-discussion}).

Functional equivalence thus leaves two questions about placement unanswered:
how long a deployment takes to converge, and which deployment best meets the non-functional requirements.
The latter is the subject of \cref{rq:deployment}.

\subsection{Language and Coordination Support}
\label{subsec:answer-languages}

To address \cref{rq:languages}, the thesis represents aggregate, choreographic, and multitier coordination
as capabilities with a shared notion of placement.
This allows one program to combine the three paradigms,
produce the components required by the deployment model,
and reject coordination errors at compile time.

\camil implements this design in Scala~3.
Its soundness proof establishes that, in a well-typed program, every communication step follows a tie in the declared architecture
and that no operation can execute under another paradigm's semantics,
since the three capability sets are disjoint (\cref{thm:camil-soundness}).
The paradigms compose through placed values, without pairwise compatibility rules (\cref{subsec:paradigm-composition}).
All 46 use cases collected from the multitier, choreographic, and aggregate literature
were reimplemented in \camil to assess its expressivity (\cref{subsec:camil-expressivity});
three further applications combine paradigms within one program (\cref{subsec:inter-paradigm}).
Placement types identify where a component's value resides,
while the architecture specifies which placements may exchange values.
Together, they supply the information required by the deployment model (\cref{sec:pulverization-abstractions}).

ScalaTropy gives choreographic and multitier programs typed operations for communication patterns
that these languages commonly express through ad-hoc constructs.
Each pattern has a distinct operation, with the target architecture recorded in the types (\cref{sec:scalatropy}).
The choice matters for communication volume:
in a matrix-vector product, the anisotropic primitive keeps the data sent by the master nearly constant as workers are added,
whereas encoding the same scatter as broadcast-and-filter makes this volume grow linearly (\cref{subsec:scalatropy-evaluation}).

These guarantees come with restrictions.
\camil rejects every nested placed scope, including those that would not deadlock,
and ScalaTropy requires the architecture to be declared before the choreography is written.

\subsubsection{Generation from Natural-Language Specifications}
\label{subsec:answer-generation}

The generation study examines a further part of \cref{rq:languages}:
whether a natural-language specification can be translated into a macro-program,
and what knowledge of the target language the model needs.
The pipeline targets aggregate programs in ScaFi.

Sixteen models were evaluated at three levels of supplied knowledge,
with generated programs checked against eleven test cases in simulation (\cref{sec:llm-pipeline}).
Without a body of knowledge, twelve models solve none of the test cases,
and most generated programs fail to compile.
A concise manual explaining the core operators' round-by-round semantics raises most compile rates above 0.90
and enables nine of those twelve models to produce successful solutions.
Supplied knowledge matters more than model size in these experiments:
the largest model leads at no knowledge level,
while adding higher-level building blocks helps some models and lowers the scores of others (\cref{sec:llm-evaluation}).

These findings concern aggregate programs in one \ac{DSL};
generation of the capability-annotated programs of \cref{chap:language-and-coordination} remains untested.
After the manual is supplied, the remaining failures concern program semantics (\cref{subsec:llm-failure-modes}).
Compilation alone is therefore insufficient, and the pipeline checks behaviour in simulation.

\subsection{Runtime Deployment Decisions}
\label{subsec:answer-deployment}

Both hand-written and learned policies can choose among admissible deployments
and revise their choices at runtime, as required by \cref{rq:deployment}.
The evaluations also show the trade-offs involved: improving a targeted non-functional quantity
can increase communication, latency, or another resource cost.

The three hand-written policies illustrate different ways to make this choice (\cref{chap:deployment-and-reconfiguration}).
Battery-triggered offloading moves an expensive component when a device's battery falls below a threshold.
The device retains more battery than in the monolithic deployment,
but the forwarding chain requires additional messages (\cref{sec:battery-triggered-offloading}).
Load-weighted fields let infrastructural devices compete for components,
increasing the proportion of application devices that offload them.
During stabilisation, however, this proportion can temporarily fall below the nearest-host baseline (\cref{sec:global-self-organising-reconfiguration}).
The energy and carbon planner searches admissible deployments against an explicit objective.
Its choices consume roughly one third of the baseline energy at every tested network size.
Reaching remotely placed units increases intra-component latency,
while latency between neighbouring devices rises only slightly (\cref{sec:declarative-green-planning}).
In each case, the operator supplies the criterion:
a threshold, a quantity for regions to compete over, or an objective and its constraints.

A learned policy derives its deployment rule from experience (\cref{chap:heterogeneous-gnn}).
Its graph representation distinguishes application devices from infrastructural devices, including edge and cloud servers,
and uses a \ac{GNN} as the Q-function (\cref{sec:hetero-graph-state}).
An aggregate density field supplies collective information absent from the individual device states (\cref{subsec:collective-information}).
Varying the scalarised reward weights produces policies at different points along the trade-off curve (\cref{subsec:idhgql-multiobjective}).
The density field makes those decisions sensitive to spatial context:
without it, the fraction of components executed locally is roughly the same regardless of device position;
with it, that fraction varies across regions and adapts when devices move between them,
without further network training (\cref{subsec:idhgql-density}).

The experiments do not establish global optimality.
A weighted sum can reach only the convex portion of the trade-off;
no choice of weights recovers deployments in a concave region of the Pareto front.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
\label{sec:future-work}

\subsection{Deployment Model}
\label{subsec:limits-model}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The deployment model assumes that component instances retain no state across rounds beyond what their inputs supply (\cref{rq:model}).
This lets one surrogate instance serve multiple owners.
Private component state would prevent instance sharing, although the theorem would still hold (\cref{sec:pulverization-discussion}).
The theorem also gives no bound on convergence time.
On hardware, only one hand-selected deployment has been implemented.
The demonstrator does not evaluate the runtime policies of \crefrange{chap:deployment-and-reconfiguration}{chap:heterogeneous-gnn},
the language support of \cref{chap:language-and-coordination}, or the generation pipeline of \cref{chap:language-based-macroprogramming},
and it provides no measurements (\cref{sec:emerge-discussion}).

\paragraph{Future work.}
Supporting stateful components requires a migration protocol and a definition of which state persists across a move.
Surrogate sharing would remain restricted to stateless components,
without changing \cref{thm:deployment-independence}.

The composable real-time specifications of Audrito et al.~\cite{DBLP:conf/isola/AudritoDT24}
offer a starting point for convergence bounds.
They bound the time after which an aggregate function's output matches its abstract model,
using network indicators such as diameter, maximum interval between rounds, communication range, and device speed.
Their minimal specification assumes fixed inputs:
if $f$ and each $g_i$ meet it with bounds $T_f$ and $T_{g_i}$,
then $f(g_1, \dots, g_n)$ meets it with bound $T_f + \max_i T_{g_i}$.
%
This composition rule mirrors the proof of \cref{thm:deployment-independence},
where upstream components stabilise before downstream ones.
Applying it along the \ac{DAG} could yield a bound at the global output ports from the bounds of individual components.
The extra hops on an offloaded component's input and output paths would make that bound depend on forwarding-chain lengths,
and therefore on the deployment.

A real-time bound is available for gradient-based distance estimation~\cite{DBLP:conf/rtss/AudritoDVB18},
one of the two services simulated in \cref{subsec:pulverization-validation}.
Its simulated convergence could be compared with the analytical bound.
The approximate and continuous specifications in the real-time framework also bound output error
when inputs change and devices move within given limits.
These could account for the transient observed during movement, which the minimal specification does not cover.
A deployment-dependent bound would let policies consider timing alongside energy or cost.

The demonstrator needs a second deployment before a policy can choose between alternatives (\cref{sec:emerge-discussion}).
Giving each robot a local runtime and replacing camera-based localisation with decentralised radio ranging
would provide a monolithic alternative to the current centralised deployment.
Communication delays in the simulation engine would then give mixed deployments a measurable cost
for the policies of \cref{chap:deployment-and-reconfiguration} or \cref{chap:heterogeneous-gnn} to optimise.
Controlled perturbation experiments on the physical devices could measure convergence times
and test the proposed analytical bounds.

\subsection{Language and Coordination Support}
\label{subsec:limits-languages}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\camil prevents deadlock with a coarse rule: it rejects all nested placed scopes,
including safe nestings permitted by existing multitier languages (\cref{rq:languages}).
ScalaTropy covers two paradigms and \camil three;
only \camil has a soundness result.
The expressivity evaluation uses examples reimplemented from the literature.
It does not measure the effort required from developers unfamiliar with the language designs.

In the generation study, the body of knowledge was refined against the same test cases used for evaluation.
The reported rates therefore measure performance within the task family used to develop the manual;
they do not establish transfer to new task families (\cref{sec:llm-macroprogramming-discussion}).
A program is scored as successful if it satisfies the assertions,
even if those assertions fail to capture the intended behaviour.
The score also omits evaluation cost.
Providers may revise hosted models without notice, so the ranking can change even with a fixed evaluation method.
The pipeline has been tested on only one \ac{DSL}.

\paragraph{Future work.}
A more precise check for nested placed scopes would admit safe programs that \camil currently rejects.
Its choreography capability could also support all four communication patterns in \cref{subsec:communication-patterns};
the design does not require point-to-point exchange to be the sole primitive (\cref{sec:language-coordination-discussion}).
ScalaTropy still needs a metatheory establishing guarantees comparable to \camil's soundness result.
For both designs, a study with developers who did not take part in their development
could compare the effort of writing the use cases with that of combining the source languages directly.

For generation, transfer should be measured on a task family written after the body of knowledge is fixed.
Comparing generated and reference execution traces across several networks
could reveal errors missed by the assertions.
Reporting tokens and attempts per solution would make the cost visible,
and fixed versions of open-weight models would make the ranking reproducible.
Extending the manual to a capability-annotated language remains an open question:
validation would need to detect placement errors as well as misinterpretations of field semantics.

\subsection{Runtime Deployment Decisions}
\label{subsec:limits-deployment}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
All deployment policies were evaluated in simulation (\cref{rq:deployment}).
Although the energy models and prices come from published measurements,
the discrepancy between simulated and physical operation has not been quantified (\cref{sec:idhgql-discussion}).
Each hand-written policy was evaluated in the setting of its own publication, with no shared workload.
The results allow comparison of the quantities improved and their associated costs,
but not a direct comparison of policy performance.
Only the field-based policy was tested on multiple topologies.

For the learned policy, reward weights express priorities and are chosen empirically, without a calibration procedure.
Application devices share a single node type,
so the representation cannot distinguish unusual battery or workload profiles.
The policies tend to place all offloaded components on servers of one kind, either edge or cloud.
Components are placed independently, which can assign two components joined by a binding to different surrogates.
Deployment independence preserves functional behaviour,
but the policy ignores the latency of the resulting forwarding chain.

\paragraph{Future work.}
Once the demonstrator supports monolithic and centralised deployments,
running a policy on it would allow simulated energy and latency to be compared with measurements.
A shared benchmark with a fixed workload, set of topologies, and energy model
would also allow a direct comparison of the three hand-written policies and the learned policy.

For the learned policy, multi-objective methods could learn a set of policies covering the Pareto front,
avoiding the convexity restriction and trial-based weight selection (\cref{sec:idhgql-discussion}).
The architecture in \cref{subsec:heterognn-q-function} is compatible with these methods.
Encoding application-device profiles would distinguish battery and workload characteristics within the existing graph.
Joint placement of bound components would use the \ac{DAG} alongside the topology,
drawing on information already present in the model of \cref{chap:pulverization}.
The reward could then account for forwarding-chain latency.

\section{Closing Remarks}
\label{sec:closing-remarks}

Component placement need not be fixed for a collective system's lifetime.
Under the conditions of \cref{thm:deployment-independence},
it can be revised at runtime to meet non-functional requirements without changing the stabilised output.
Putting this freedom into practice requires self-stabilising components,
uniform sensing along forwarding chains, and enough time for convergence between revisions.
Checking these conditions across the languages and policies developed here
is the next step towards a system that can make and revise its own deployment decisions.
